Data, Variables and Methods

Data

This research draws on the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a national panel study fielded between 2016 and 2023 to examine attitudes, emotions, and behaviors among adults aged 18 to 75 in relation to social cohesion and conflict. ELSOC uses a probabilistic, stratified, clustered, multistage sampling design covering urban centers and small towns. The sampling frame is proportionally stratified into six categories of urban population size; within these strata, households were selected through multistage procedures based on 1,067 sampled blocks.

ELSOC has been administered annually since 2016, except in 2020 when fieldwork was suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic. For this study, we use waves 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2022, and 2023. The 2021 wave is excluded because the questionnaire version omitted key variables for the analysis. By wave 6, cumulative panel attrition was approximately 40%; we therefore restrict the analysis to a balanced panel, yielding a two-level data set of N = 7,133 observations nested within N = 1,210 individuals. Additional documentation on sampling, attrition, and weighting is available from the ELSOC study team, and the data are publicly accessible through the ELSOC Dataverse.

Variables

Market justice preferences

The dependent variable in this study is market justice preferences (MJP). This construct is measured by three items that capture how strongly individuals endorse conditioning access to pensions, education and health care on income. The original items asks: “Is it fair that high-income individuals have better (education, health or pensions) than lower-income individuals?”. The response options range from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), so that higher values indicate stronger support for market-justified inequality in the provision of social services.

Static Social Subjective Status

Subjective social status is measured with three ELSOC items in which respondents place themselves (own), their family of origin (origin) and their children (children) on a scale from 0 (“lowest”) to 10 (“highest”) of the social ladder. The first item captures the respondent’s current position (own SSS), the second the position of the family of origin (origin SSS), and the third the position where respondents expect their children to be located (children’s SSS). Higher values indicate a higher perceived position in all three cases. The three measures are treated as continuous variables and, in the models, are decomposed into their within-person and between-person components.

Dinamic Subjective Social Status

Based on the original variables of subjective social status (origin, own and children), a variable were generated from all the combinations betweeen origin and children SSS items. The composite variable operationalize subjective mobility as follows: \(t_{2} - t_{1}\). Thus, positive values indicate upward subjective mobility.The variable is treated as continuous and, in the models, is decomposed into their within-person and between-person components. Static Social Objective Status

Objective social status is measured through education and household income. Education is the respondent’s highest educational level, reported on a ten-category scale ranging from no schooling to postgraduate studies. For analysis we use a dichotomous indicator of university education (university or postgraduate vs. lower levels).

Income is the total monthly household income reported by the respondent. When the amount is missing, we impute the midpoint of the income bracket answered in the follow-up question. Household income is deflated to constant December 2023 prices using the consumer price index and divided by the square root of household size to obtain an equivalised income. For analysis we constructed a dichotomous indicator for the top decile (top decile vs lower deciles).

Dinamic Social Objective Status

The income trajectory compares where each person stood in the income distribution at the beginning of the panel (origin) with where they stand at the end (destination). It is a person-fixed variable: it takes the same value in all waves of each individual. The Table 2 provides a more detailed description of how this variable was calculated.

Controls

Based on previous research (Castillo et al. 2025; Lindh 2015; Jaime-Castillo 2013), the statistical models include sociodemographic and attitudinal controls to account for potential compositional effects in the population. We adjust for sex (1 = male, 2 = female), age (in years) and political identification (1 = left, 2 = centre, 3 = right, 4 = no identification).

Analytical Strategy

The analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we describe the distribution of each status variable by wave and examine the bivariate association between the status measures and market justice preferences using Spearman correlations, computed within each wave and, for the within/between-person components, on the pooled sample. Second, we estimate linear longitudinal multilevel models for the market justice preferences (MJP) index, which is the mean of the pension, education and health items (range 1-5). Because the index is an average of several items and is treated as a continuous outcome, we use linear mixed models rather than cumulative link models. This approach accounts for the hierarchical structure of the panel data, with repeated observations (Level 1) nested within individuals (Level 2), and enables the decomposition of the variance of the predictors into within-person (WE) and between-person (BE) components, allowing us to distinguish temporal changes within individuals from stable differences between individuals (Singer 2003; Bell, Fairbrother, and Jones 2019). All descriptive figures, correlations and models are based on the same analytical sample (complete cases on all analysis variables; N = 7,133 observations from N = 1,210 individuals).

Within-Person and Between-Person Random Effects Models

Following the recommendations of Bell and Jones (2015), we decompose time-varying predictors into their within-person and between-person components using person-mean centering:

\[ X_{WE_{it}} = X_{it} - \bar{X}_i \]

\[ X_{BE_i} = \bar{X}_i \]

where \(X_{it}\) represents the observed value for individual \(i\) at time \(t\), \(\bar{X}_i\) is the person-specific mean across all observations, \(X_{WE_{it}}\) captures within-person deviations from the individual mean (representing temporal fluctuations), and \(X_{BE_i}\) represents stable between-person differences (time-invariant characteristics). The decomposition is applied to the continuous measures: the three subjective status measures (origin, own and children) and the subjective mobility.

For the dichotomous and categorical measures of objective status only a between-person component is included, since they vary little within individuals. Therefore, the BE component indicates whether the person was ever observed with a university degree or in the top income decile.

This decomposition allows us to distinguish two theoretically distinct processes: (1) Within-person effects capture how changes in an individual’s subjective and objective status over time relate to changes in their market justice preferences, controlling for stable individual characteristics. (2) Between-person effects capture how individuals with different average levels of subjective and objective status differ in their market justice preferences, net of temporal fluctuations.

Model Specification

The general form of our linear mixed model is:

\[ \text{MJP}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{wave}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{wave}^2_{it} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_3' X_{WE_{it}} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_4' X_{BE_i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' Z_i + u_{0i} + u_{1i} \text{wave}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

where \(\text{MJP}_{it}\) is the market justice preferences index for individual \(i\) at wave \(t\); \(\beta_0\) is the fixed intercept; \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) capture the linear and quadratic time trends (wave is coded 1 to 6, since the 2020-2021 waves are not available); \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_3\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_4\) are the within-person and between-person effects of the status measures included in each model; \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) are the coefficients of the controls \(Z_i\) (sex, age and political identification), which enter only in the models that include controls; \(u_{0i}\) is the random intercept (capturing unobserved individual heterogeneity); \(u_{1i}\) is a random slope for wave (allowing individual-specific temporal trajectories); and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is the residual term, \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\).

The random effects are assumed to follow a bivariate normal distribution:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} u_{0i} \\ u_{1i} \end{pmatrix} \sim N \begin{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2_{u_0} & \sigma_{u_{01}} \\ \sigma_{u_{01}} & \sigma^2_{u_1} \end{pmatrix} \end{pmatrix} \]

Model Building Strategy

All models share the same base specification: the linear and quadratic time terms, a random intercept and a random slope for wave, \((1 + \text{wave} \mid \text{individual})\). They are estimated by maximum likelihood (not REML), so that models with different fixed effects can be compared through likelihood-ratio tests (LRT). The income trajectory enters as a categorical predictor with the reference category Stable low; individuals without a valid trajectory are kept in the sample as an additional Missings category, so that no observations are lost. Whenever controls are included, they are sex, age, political identification and the between-person component of university education. All status and mobility measures enter through their within-person (WE) and between-person (BE) components. We build the models in two steps, first without and then with interactions, and repeat each step for the subjective status measures and for the subjective mobility measure.

Step 1: main effects of subjective status/mobility and income trajectory. We estimate nested models in which the trajectory enters only as a main effect:

  1. Model 1 includes only the time terms and the income trajectory.
  2. Model 2 (base) adds the controls.
  3. In the status models, Models 3 to 5 add to the base model, one at a time, the WE and BE components of each subjective status (origin, own and children). The blocks are not entered cumulatively, so each model isolates the contribution of a single status measure. In the mobility models, Model 3 adds the WE and BE components of the subjective mobility between children and origin (mov_exp_og) to the base model.

Each block of status or mobility is compared with the base model, and each model is compared with the same model without the trajectory, through LRT. In this step the random-effects structure is the one described above.

Step 2: interactions between income trajectory and subjective status/mobility. To test whether the association between subjective status (or mobility) and market justice preferences depends on the income trajectory, we interact the trajectory with the WE and the BE component of the measure:

\[ \text{MJP}_{it} = \dots + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1' (\text{Trajectory}_i \times X_{WE_{it}}) + \boldsymbol{\delta}_2' (\text{Trajectory}_i \times X_{BE_i}) + u_{0i} + u_{1i}\,\text{wave}_{it} + u_{2i} X_{WE_{it}} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

We estimate one model for each subjective status (origin, own and children), which includes the controls, the six main effects of status (WE and BE), the trajectory and the interactions of the trajectory with the WE and the BE of the status of that model. A separate model, with the same structure, is estimated for the subjective mobility. Because the interaction with the WE component assumes that the within-person association may vary across individuals, in these models the slope of the interacted WE component is also random, \((1 + \text{wave} + X_{WE} \mid \text{individual})\), which adds the term \(u_{2i} X_{WE_{it}}\) to the specification.

For each interaction model we report three LRT: (i) the whole block of interactions (WE and BE) against a base model with the same random slope but no interactions; (ii) the interactions with the WE component only against that same base model; and (iii) the model with the random slope against the model without it. The coefficients of the interactions are differences with respect to the reference category (Stable low), and the slope of each trajectory is the sum of the main effect and its interaction. Since the BE components are not centred, the main effects of the trajectory are read at a BE value of zero, which almost nobody has, and are not interpretable on their own. The categories with very few individuals (Low -> Top and Top -> Low) yield unstable estimates and should be interpreted with caution.
