Introduction

In recent decades, welfare states have undergone significant transformations in their institutional architecture, with social services no longer being exclusively public but coexisting with private alternatives (Busemeyer and Iversen 2020). As a result, market dynamics have taken root around the world in services such as education, health care, and pension systems. However, market-oriented policies have not only formal consequences but also cultural ones, operating as a moral project in which individuals internalize these market values and adopt them as normative criteria (Fourcade and Healy 2007). In this context, the concept of “market justice” has become key to understanding how people justify the fact that access to and the quality of social services depend on market criteria such as the ability to pay.

The literature has extensively examined how preferences for market justice relate to objective status, taking into account characteristics such as education, income, and occupational status. On this topic, the results are inconsistent: (Lindh 2015) found that those from a higher social class tend to support market-oriented distribution, while (Castillo et al. 2026) found no significant differences between class position and market justice regarding pensions. It appears that one’s position in the social structure is not sufficient on its own to explain these beliefs; yet, self-perceived status has played a minor role in the study of market preferences, despite evidence of the effect of subjective social status—for example, on perceptions of inequality and the demand for redistribution (Melli and Azzollini 2025b, 2025a). Along these lines, it is necessary to incorporate the subjective dimension of social stratification into the analysis, as it can complement what is provided by objective status while also broadening the explanatory framework of market justice.

However, self-perception within the social structure is not static; rather, it can also reflect how people assess their trajectories—whether upward or downward—at a given moment, as well as how this perception changes over time. Within this framework, subjective mobility takes on particular significance, as it allows for the study of status in broader terms than occupational attainment—which is used to measure objective mobility—and, along the same lines, can provide greater insights than standardized measures (Duru-Bellat and Kieffer 2008; Mijs et al. 2022).

To date, only a couple of sociological studies have addressed the role of mobility in its subjective dimension (Bar-Haim 2018; Gugushvili 2021), but to the best of our knowledge, none has rigorously examined the link between objective and subjective status, and how changes in these statuses over time affect preferences for market justice. To study subjective intergenerational mobility, some studies have asked whether respondents “have done better than their parents,” measured using a Likert scale of agreement, without clearly defining the context in which a comparative improvement relative to the family of origin would be expected (Gugushvili 2021). Furthermore, this creates a conflict when most measures of one’s own subjective social status are based on the McArthur or TopBot scales (Anderson 2025). On the other hand, the inability to study the evolution of these beliefs over time stems from a lack of panel data capable of measuring them from a longitudinal perspective.

This study aims to contribute to the literature on market justice and social stratification in two ways. First, through a rigorous examination of objective social status and, above all, subjective social status. This results in an innovation in the operationalization of subjective social status, made possible by the data used. In addition to one’s own subjective social status, we include that of the family of origin (past) and that of one’s children (future expectation); based on this, we generate a series of variables to analytically represent subjective social mobility and thus observe how they relate to market justice. Second, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to address the interaction between objective and subjective social status and how they change over time. Beyond studying the factors that determine market justice, we focus on how support for market-based criteria in social services varies over time, and to what extent objective and subjective social mobility can explain these changes. Our main argument is that subjective social status (both cross-sectionally and in terms of mobility) will carry the same or greater weight than objective status, shedding new light on how the perception of one’s own life trajectory plays a fundamental role in market-oriented criteria of justice.

This research is particularly relevant in Chile, a country characterized by a model marked by high levels of inequality and rigid social mobility (Flores et al. 2020; Torche 2014), the foundations of which took shape during the civil-military dictatorship that the country endured between 1973 and 1989 (Madariaga 2020). Similarly, there is a high degree of privatization in areas of social reproduction, coupled with a strong dependence on the state (Boccardo 2020). In addition, we have the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a unique project in Latin America that tracked and recorded people’s characteristics and beliefs between 2016 and 2023, allowing us to study change over time. Thus, these conditions make the study of Chile a productive case for understanding the extent to which status (both objective and subjective) determines preferences for market justice.
