Abstract

Contemporary welfare states are characterized by the public provision of services such as education, health care, and pensions. Such services have experienced a process of commodification over the last years, whereby access to public goods increasingly depends on individual payment capacity, linked to the concept of market justice (Lane, 1986; Lindh, 2015). In this context, the study of market justice preferences acquires relevance as support for that type of distribution undermines the core of the welfare state.

Previous literature has found modest support for the role of class and status variables in shaping market justice preferences within a self-interest logic, usually from a static (cross-sectional) perspective. In the present paper, we include different measures of subjective social status, arguing that changes in this dimension operate above and beyond objective status measures.

Furthermore, we add a dynamic perspective, arguing that increasing objective and subjective status over time will be associated with stronger endorsement of market-justice preferences. This study draws on data from the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a representative panel of the Chilean urban population with six waves from 2016 to 2023 (N = 1326). This unique dataset offers, for the first time, the possibility to analyze variations across three dimensions of subjective social status: own, family of origin, and (future) offspring. The results of the multilevel longitudinal models indicate that subjective status is positively associated with market justice preferences (ceteris paribus). Moreover, the association between status variables and market justice preferences is far from being static, such that increasing status leads to stronger support for the role of individual payment capacity in accessing public goods.

Keywords: Longitudinal multilevel models, Market justice preferences, Social stratification, Social subjective status

Introduction

The allocation of public goods and services is a central concern of social justice. When health care, education, and pensions are provided by the state, the distribution of these goods is often based on principles of equality and need. However, when these services are provided through market mechanisms, access and quality may depend on individuals’ ability to pay, leading to a different set of justice criteria. The concepts of political justice and market justice have been developed to describe these two distinct approaches to the distribution of social goods (Lane, 1986). But, could the market be considered a fair mechanism for distributing social goods? This question has been the subject of extensive debate in the literature on social justice, with some arguing that market mechanisms can be efficient and fair, while others contend that they exacerbate inequality and undermine social cohesion (Fourcade & Healy, 2007; Lindh, 2015). Research on attitudes toward market justice has shown that, although there is a general preference for political justice, there is also a significant share of the population that supports market-oriented distribution, particularly in the case of pensions (Castillo et al., 2026). This raises important questions about the factors that shape individuals’ preferences for market justice and how these preferences may change over time.

Social status has been usually considered a key factor in understanding social justice attitudes, as market justice preferences. Objective status variables such as education, income, and occupation have been related to preferences for market justice mechanisms, consisten with a rational approach (Lindh, 2015). Regarding subjective social status –the self-assessment of one’s position within the social hierarchy – there is evidence that it plays a significant role in shaping individuals’ beliefs about fairness and justice (Bar-Haim, 2018; Iturra-Sanhueza et al., 2026; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a, 2025b). However, the relationship between subjective status and market justice preferences has been less explored, and the existing literature has produced mixed findings. The present paper aims at disentangling the relationship between objective and subjective social status and their relationship with market justice preferences. Consistent with previous socio-psychological research on subjective social status and inequality attitudes (Bar-Haim, 2018; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a, 2025b), we argue that subjective social status (net of objective status) entails identity and self-perception processes that shape individuals’ beliefs about fairness and justice above and beyond their objective circumstances.

A second important aspect to take into account when studying the relationship between social status and market justice preferences is the dynamic nature of social status. Social mobility, or the ability of individuals to move up or down the social ladder, has been a central concern of social stratification research. Traditional approaches have focused on objective measures of social mobility, such as changes in income, education, and occupation across generations. However, recent research has highlighted the importance of subjective social mobility, which refers to individuals’ perceptions of their own social mobility and how it affects their beliefs about fairness and justice (Hiltner & Cech, 2024). This perspective suggests that individuals’ perceptions of their own life trajectory may be more important than their actual movements within the labor market, as it reflects how people assess their trajectories—whether upward or downward—at a given moment, as well as how this perception changes over time.

For the present study, we use six waves of panel data from the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey 2016-2023 (ELSOC) to examine the relationship between objective and subjective social status and market justice preferences. Our analysis focuses on two main research questions: (1) How do objective and subjective social status relate to market justice preferences? and (2) How do these relationships change over time, particularly in the context of social mobility? By addressing these questions, we aim to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the status factors that shape individuals’ beliefs about fairness and justice in market-oriented societies.

This research is particularly relevant in Chile, a country characterized by a model marked by high levels of inequality and rigid social mobility (Flores et al., 2020; Torche, 2014), the foundations of which took shape during the civil-military dictatorship that the country endured between 1973 and 1989 (Madariaga, 2020). Similarly, there is a high degree of privatization in areas of social reproduction, coupled with a strong dependence on the state (Boccardo, 2020). In addition, we have the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a unique project in Latin America that tracked and recorded people’s characteristics and beliefs between 2016 and 2023, allowing us to study change over time. Thus, these conditions make the study of Chile a productive case for understanding the extent to which status (both objective and subjective) determines preferences for market justice.

Market justice preferences

The study of attitudes towards allocation of social goods is a central topic in the literature on social justice as well as in welfare state research (Van Oorschot et al. (2017); Svallfors (2007)). On the one hand, the literature on social justice has focused on the moral and ethical principles that guide individuals’ beliefs about fairness and justice in society. On the other hand, welfare state research has examined how these beliefs are shaped by institutional arrangements and policy contexts. In this context, most of the research has focused on the study of attitudes towards redistribution, which refers to the transfer of resources from one group to another in order to reduce inequality and promote social welfare. However, there is a growing interest in understanding how individuals perceive the role of markets in the distribution of social goods and services, which is referred to as market justice preferences.

The role of markets in the distribution of social goods has been a topic of debate in the literature on social justice and welfare state research. The term commodification it is used to describe the process by which goods and services that were previously provided as public goods are transformed into commodities that can be bought and sold in the market (Fourcade & Healy, 2007). This process has been associated with a shift towards market-oriented policies, which prioritize efficiency and individual choice over social welfare and equity. In this context, market justice preferences refer to individuals’ beliefs about the fairness of market-based distribution of social goods and services (Lane, 1986), such as education, health care, and pensions.

Regarding the operationalization of market justice preferences, survey research has typically focused on the measurement of attitudes towards the provision of social goods and services based on individuals’ ability to pay in different policy domain. This agenda has been developed in the context of the International Social Survey Programme (ISSP), which has included questions on market justice preferences in its surveys on social inequality, in particular related to the justification of access to better education and health care based on individuals’ ability to pay (Immergut & Schneider, 2020; Lindh, 2015). Research is this area has found that market justice preferences are associated with individual-level factors, such as socioeconomic status and political ideology, as well as contextual factors, such as the level of privatization in the provision of social goods and services. However, there is still a need for further research to understand the complex interplay between these factors and how they shape individuals’ beliefs about market justice.

Subjective social status

Subjective social status (SSS) refers to individuals’ perception of their own position in the social hierarchy (M. D. R. Evans & Kelley, 2004; Jackman & Jackman, 1973). Because information about the overall distribution is limited, people rely on heuristics to establish their own position, drawing on a subjective sample of their close reference groups (Cruces, Pérez Truglia, et al., 2013; M. D. R. Evans et al., 1992). Rather than mirroring objective resources, SSS results from a social comparison process: people assess their standing against the reference groups available to them, typically family, friends, neighbours and co-workers (M. D. R. Evans et al., 1992; Kelley & Evans, 1995). Because personal networks are strongly homophilous in education, occupation and income (McPherson et al., 2001), these reference groups are a socially skewed sample of the wider distribution. The result is a well-documented compression towards the middle: those at the bottom tend to overestimate their position, those at the top to underestimate it, and most people identify with the middle (Castillo et al., 2013; Kelley & Evans, 1995). What is observed as SSS is therefore a blend of one’s objective position and the position of those against whom it is judged. The literature refers to the concept as subjective social status, subjective social position (Lindemann & Saar, 2014), subjective social location (M. D. R. Evans & Kelley, 2004) or subjective evaluation of social status (Słomczyński & Kacprowicz, 1986), and measures it in different ways. The most widespread instrument is the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status, a ten-rung ladder on which respondents place themselves relative to others in society (Adler et al., 2000), alongside analogous top–bottom self-placement items in comparative surveys such as the ISSP (Anderson & Aussant, 2025; M. D. R. Evans & Kelley, 2004).

SSS is systematically patterned by individual and contextual socioeconomic factors. At the individual level, income, educational attainment and occupational status are consistently associated with higher self-placement (M. A. Andersson, 2018; Chen & Williams, 2018; Iturra & Mellado, 2018). More recently, attention has turned to the content of work. Across 25 European countries, A. B. Andersson & Lindh (2023) show that a substantial share of the variation in SSS lies between occupations, that the occupational status gradient closely follows the educational requirements of jobs, and that non-manual work carries an additional status premium over manual work. At the interpersonal level, network composition also matters: knowing more people in high-status occupations is associated with higher SSS (Kim & Lee, 2021), and related evidence on subjective class identification shows the same pattern (Huang, 2021). At the contextual level, economic prosperity tends to raise SSS and make societies appear more egalitarian (M. D. R. Evans & Kelley, 2017; Lindemann & Saar, 2014; Wiesner & Iturra-Sanhueza, 2025), while income inequality depresses it, particularly among the working class, and distorts how people locate themselves in the distribution (Nolan & Weisstanner, 2022; Wiesner & Iturra-Sanhueza, 2025).

SSS is in turn linked to attitudes towards economic inequality, particularly distributive perceptions and preferences. People who place themselves higher tend to perceive smaller income gaps and a less unequal social structure (Castillo et al., 2022; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a), to attribute poverty to individual rather than structural causes (Schneider & Castillo, 2015), to legitimise existing inequalities (Buchel et al., 2021), to perceive society as more meritocratic (Iturra-Sanhueza et al., 2026) and to support redistribution less (Brown-Iannuzzi et al., 2015; Duman, 2020; Kirsten et al., 2022). A recurrent finding is that SSS explains these attitudes over and above objective position precisely because it captures the social comparison mechanism; when objective and subjective status diverge, the latter tends to prevail (Iturra-Sanhueza et al., 2026; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a). Research has nonetheless focused on respondents’ own SSS, leaving largely unexplored how people evaluate the status of their reference groups. The reference group and reality blend hypothesis holds that status perceptions are anchored in the social position of close ties such as friends and family members (M. D. R. Evans & Kelley, 2004; Kelley & Evans, 1995). In a study of SSS and perceived meritocracy in Chile, Iturra-Sanhueza (2022) shows that the association with one’s own SSS weakens once the subjective status of the family of origin and, especially, the expected future status of one’s children are taken into account. Higher expected status for one’s children is associated with lower perceived meritocracy, a pattern interpreted as relative deprivation stemming from spurious mobility: dissatisfaction with status attainment arising from a perceived mismatch between efforts and rewards. Market justice preferences, the view that access to social services such as education, healthcare and pensions may legitimately depend on income (Castillo et al., 2025), remain particularly underexplored in this respect. Little is known about how one’s own SSS relates to them, and even less about the role of the subjective status of close ties, leaving a gap still to be addressed.

Social Status and Mobility

Objective and subjective social status

Research on the social foundations of distributional preferences has traditionally relied on objective indicators of social position—such as income, education, and occupational class—and on a logic of self-interest, according to which those who benefit least from redistribution tend to support it the least (Alesina et al., 2018; Lindh & McCall, 2020). A complementary tradition focuses on subjective social status (SSS), understood as the position people believe they occupy in the social hierarchy (Adler et al., 2000). Both dimensions are related, but they are far from interchangeable, as the accumulated evidence shows that their correlation is only moderate (Tan et al., 2020).

However, the distance between these two dimensions is not random. People assess their position by comparing themselves to their reference groups, which tends to shift their self-placement toward the center of the hierarchy, both among those at the top and those at the bottom (G. Evans et al., 2022; Lindemann & Saar, 2014). In the case of Chile, one of the most unequal countries, this compression is particularly pronounced, especially among those with higher objective status (Castillo et al., 2013). Consequently, subjective status may contain its own information, distinct from objective position, but difficult to capture based solely on its levels: using the same data from this study, what predicts participation in the 2019 protests is not subjective status itself, but rather its mismatch with objective status (Bargain et al., 2026). In other words, the more people perceive themselves as lower in the hierarchy than their objective position would suggest, the more likely they are to participate in collective action.

Subjective social status and distributive preferences

A growing body of literature argues that the subjective dimension of status matters for distributive attitudes beyond objective resources. The central mechanism is social comparison: people evaluate the fairness of a distribution based on where they believe they stand relative to others, such that feeling lower in the hierarchy increases support for redistribution, even when actual resources remain unchanged. Experimental evidence supports this causal interpretation (Brown-Iannuzzi et al., 2015; Condon & Wichowsky, 2020; Cruces, Perez-Truglia, et al., 2013), and comparative survey evidence shows that subjective status predicts support for redistribution and perceived inequality independently of objective class—and even more strongly than objective class does (Choi, 2021; Duman, 2019; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a, 2025b).

Two caveats qualify this picture. First, the subjective gradient depends on the context. In the most unequal countries, people with high subjective status also show greater support for redistribution, which reduces the gap between status groups (Melli & Azzollini, 2025a, 2025b). Highly unequal and strongly market-oriented contexts, such as Chile, could therefore be cases where subjective status contributes less than expected. Second, most of this evidence is cross-sectional and relies on differences between individuals. Panel studies suggest that redistributive preferences are quite stable, anchored in long-term economic position and early socialization, and respond only to major changes in circumstances (O’Grady, 2019). Even major shocks, such as job loss, produce changes that dissipate once the shock ends (Margalit, 2013), although views on the justice of inequality do shift in response to individual events such as unemployment or a promotion (Hvidberg et al., 2023). Therefore, the question remains open as to whether the association between status and distributive preferences reflects stable differences between individuals or an adjustment within each individual as their status changes, which requires distinguishing between intrapersonal variation and interpersonality variation (Bell & Jones, 2015).

Past and expected mobility

Status is not just a position, but also a trajectory: where people come from and where they hope to go can matter just as much as where they are. The literature on mobility has suggested that prospects for upward mobility can moderate demands for redistribution (Alesina et al., 2018; Benabou & Ok, 2001) and that, as with status, perceived mobility has greater attitudinal consequences than objective mobility (Gugushvili, 2016; Hiltner & Cech, 2024).

These effects, however, are not uniform. They vary depending on the type of policy or social service in question (Gugushvili, 2019), and when mobility is measured objectively, people tend to adopt the preferences of their destination position rather than those of their origin (Jaime‐Castillo & Marqués‐Perales, 2019).

Expectations about the future, in particular, seem to carry more weight than past mobility (Schuck & Shore, 2019), but the direction of their effect depends on how that upward mobility is expected to be achieved. If the next generation’s prospects are tied to public provision, optimism regarding one’s children may be accompanied by greater support for public services and less acceptance of the idea that access should depend on the ability to pay (Cojocaru, 2014).

Status, Mobility and Inequality Preferences

These debates have rarely been applied to market justice preferences—that is, to the idea that it is fair for access to services such as pensions, health care, and education to depend on the ability to pay. Comparative evidence suggests that these preferences are reinforced where private provision is most widespread (Immergut & Schneider, 2020; Lindh, 2015), making Chile—with its widely commercialized pensions, health care, and education—a paradigmatic case. Previous studies using the same panel data have examined the role of perceived inequality Castillo et al. (2026), but the roles of objective and subjective status—and how they change over time—have not been addressed jointly.

The question therefore remains open as to whether market justice preferences respond to the position people occupy, the one they believe they occupy, or the one they hope to attain—and whether they do so as stable differences between individuals or as changes within each individual. Answering this requires measuring subjective status in its past, present, and future dimensions (origin, self, and children) and separating these two sources of variation.

Data, Variables and Methods

Data

This research draws on the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a national panel study fielded between 2016 and 2023 to examine attitudes, emotions, and behaviors among adults aged 18 to 75 in relation to social cohesion and conflict. ELSOC uses a probabilistic, stratified, clustered, multistage sampling design covering urban centers and small towns. The sampling frame is proportionally stratified into six categories of urban population size; within these strata, households were selected through multistage procedures based on 1,067 sampled blocks.

ELSOC has been administered annually since 2016, except in 2020 when fieldwork was suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic. For this study, we use waves 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2022, and 2023. The 2021 wave is excluded because the questionnaire version omitted key variables for the analysis. By wave 6, cumulative panel attrition was approximately 40%; we therefore restrict the analysis to a balanced panel, yielding a two-level data set of N = 7,133 observations nested within N = 1,210 individuals. Additional documentation on sampling, attrition, and weighting is available from the ELSOC study team, and the data are publicly accessible through the ELSOC Dataverse.

Variables

Market justice preferences

The dependent variable in this study is market justice preferences (MJP). This construct is measured by three items that capture how strongly individuals endorse conditioning access to pensions, education and health care on income. The original items asks: “Is it fair that high-income individuals have better (education, health or pensions) than lower-income individuals?”. The response options range from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), so that higher values indicate stronger support for market-justified inequality in the provision of social services.

Therefore, we construct the MJP index as the mean of the three items, which ranges from 1 to 5. To validate the index, we estimated the Spearman correlations between the three market justice items (see Figure 2) and computed Cronbach’s alpha to assess their internal consistency (0.83). The index is computed only for respondents with valid answers on all three items and is treated as a continuous variable in the models.

Static Social Subjective Status

Subjective social status is measured with three ELSOC items in which respondents place themselves (own), their family of origin (origin) and their children (children) on a scale from 0 (“lowest”) to 10 (“highest”) of the social ladder. The first item captures the respondent’s current position (own SSS), the second the position of the family of origin (origin SSS), and the third the position where respondents expect their children to be located (children’s SSS). Higher values indicate a higher perceived position in all three cases. The three measures are treated as continuous variables and, in the models, are decomposed into their within-person and between-person components.

Dinamic Subjective Social Status

Based on the original variables of subjective social status (origin, own and children), a variable were generated from all the combinations betweeen origin and children SSS items. The composite variable operationalize subjective mobility as follows: \(t_{2} - t_{1}\). Thus, positive values indicate upward subjective mobility.The variable is treated as continuous and, in the models, is decomposed into their within-person and between-person components. Static Social Objective Status

Objective social status is measured through education and household income. Education is the respondent’s highest educational level, reported on a ten-category scale ranging from no schooling to postgraduate studies. For analysis we use a dichotomous indicator of university education (university or postgraduate vs. lower levels).

Income is the total monthly household income reported by the respondent. When the amount is missing, we impute the midpoint of the income bracket answered in the follow-up question. Household income is deflated to constant December 2023 prices using the consumer price index and divided by the square root of household size to obtain an equivalised income. For analysis we constructed a dichotomous indicator for the top decile (top decile vs lower deciles).

Analytical Strategy

The analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we describe the distribution of each status variable by wave and examine the bivariate association between the status measures and market justice preferences using Spearman correlations, computed within each wave and, for the within/between-person components, on the pooled sample. Second, we estimate linear longitudinal multilevel models for the market justice preferences (MJP) index, which is the mean of the pension, education and health items (range 1-5). Because the index is an average of several items and is treated as a continuous outcome, we use linear mixed models rather than cumulative link models. This approach accounts for the hierarchical structure of the panel data, with repeated observations (Level 1) nested within individuals (Level 2), and enables the decomposition of the variance of the predictors into within-person (WE) and between-person (BE) components, allowing us to distinguish temporal changes within individuals from stable differences between individuals (Bell et al., 2019; Singer, 2003). All descriptive figures, correlations and models are based on the same analytical sample (complete cases on all analysis variables; N = 7,133 observations from N = 1,210 individuals).

Within-Person and Between-Person Random Effects Models

Following the recommendations of Bell & Jones (2015), we decompose time-varying predictors into their within-person and between-person components using person-mean centering:

\[ X_{WE_{it}} = X_{it} - \bar{X}_i \]

\[ X_{BE_i} = \bar{X}_i \]

where \(X_{it}\) represents the observed value for individual \(i\) at time \(t\), \(\bar{X}_i\) is the person-specific mean across all observations, \(X_{WE_{it}}\) captures within-person deviations from the individual mean (representing temporal fluctuations), and \(X_{BE_i}\) represents stable between-person differences (time-invariant characteristics). The decomposition is applied to the continuous measures: the three subjective status measures (origin, own and children) and the subjective mobility.

For the dichotomous and categorical measures of objective status only a between-person component is included, since they vary little within individuals. Therefore, the BE component indicates whether the person was ever observed with a university degree or in the top income decile.

This decomposition allows us to distinguish two theoretically distinct processes: (1) Within-person effects capture how changes in an individual’s subjective and objective status over time relate to changes in their market justice preferences, controlling for stable individual characteristics. (2) Between-person effects capture how individuals with different average levels of subjective and objective status differ in their market justice preferences, net of temporal fluctuations.

Model Specification

The general form of our linear mixed model is:

\[ \text{MJP}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{wave}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{wave}^2_{it} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_3' X_{WE_{it}} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_4' X_{BE_i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' Z_i + u_{0i} + u_{1i} \text{wave}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

where \(\text{MJP}_{it}\) is the market justice preferences index for individual \(i\) at wave \(t\); \(\beta_0\) is the fixed intercept; \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) capture the linear and quadratic time trends (wave is coded 1 to 6, since the 2020-2021 waves are not available); \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_3\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_4\) are the within-person and between-person effects of the status measures included in each model; \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) are the coefficients of the controls \(Z_i\) (sex, age and political identification), which enter only in the models that include controls; \(u_{0i}\) is the random intercept (capturing unobserved individual heterogeneity); \(u_{1i}\) is a random slope for wave (allowing individual-specific temporal trajectories); and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is the residual term, \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\).

The random effects are assumed to follow a bivariate normal distribution:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} u_{0i} \\ u_{1i} \end{pmatrix} \sim N \begin{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2_{u_0} & \sigma_{u_{01}} \\ \sigma_{u_{01}} & \sigma^2_{u_1} \end{pmatrix} \end{pmatrix} \]

Model Building Strategy

All models share the same base specification: the linear and quadratic time terms, a random intercept and a random slope for wave, \((1 + \text{wave} \mid \text{individual})\). They are estimated by maximum likelihood (not REML), so that models with different fixed effects can be compared through likelihood-ratio tests (LRT). The income trajectory enters as a categorical predictor with the reference category Stable low; individuals without a valid trajectory are kept in the sample as an additional Missings category, so that no observations are lost. Whenever controls are included, they are sex, age, political identification and the between-person component of university education. All status and mobility measures enter through their within-person (WE) and between-person (BE) components. We build the models in two steps, first without and then with interactions, and repeat each step for the subjective status measures and for the subjective mobility measure.

Step 1: main effects of subjective status/mobility and income trajectory. We estimate nested models in which the trajectory enters only as a main effect:

  1. Model 1 includes only the time terms and the income trajectory.
  2. Model 2 (base) adds the controls.
  3. In the status models, Models 3 to 5 add to the base model, one at a time, the WE and BE components of each subjective status (origin, own and children). The blocks are not entered cumulatively, so each model isolates the contribution of a single status measure. In the mobility models, Model 3 adds the WE and BE components of the subjective mobility between children and origin (mov_exp_og) to the base model.

Each block of status or mobility is compared with the base model, and each model is compared with the same model without the trajectory, through LRT. In this step the random-effects structure is the one described above.

Step 2: interactions between income trajectory and subjective status/mobility. To test whether the association between subjective status (or mobility) and market justice preferences depends on the income trajectory, we interact the trajectory with the WE and the BE component of the measure:

\[ \text{MJP}_{it} = \dots + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1' (\text{Trajectory}_i \times X_{WE_{it}}) + \boldsymbol{\delta}_2' (\text{Trajectory}_i \times X_{BE_i}) + u_{0i} + u_{1i}\,\text{wave}_{it} + u_{2i} X_{WE_{it}} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

We estimate one model for each subjective status (origin, own and children), which includes the controls, the six main effects of status (WE and BE), the trajectory and the interactions of the trajectory with the WE and the BE of the status of that model. A separate model, with the same structure, is estimated for the subjective mobility. Because the interaction with the WE component assumes that the within-person association may vary across individuals, in these models the slope of the interacted WE component is also random, \((1 + \text{wave} + X_{WE} \mid \text{individual})\), which adds the term \(u_{2i} X_{WE_{it}}\) to the specification.

For each interaction model we report three LRT: (i) the whole block of interactions (WE and BE) against a base model with the same random slope but no interactions; (ii) the interactions with the WE component only against that same base model; and (iii) the model with the random slope against the model without it. The coefficients of the interactions are differences with respect to the reference category (Stable low), and the slope of each trajectory is the sum of the main effect and its interaction. Since the BE components are not centred, the main effects of the trajectory are read at a BE value of zero, which almost nobody has, and are not interpretable on their own. The categories with very few individuals (Low -> Top and Top -> Low) yield unstable estimates and should be interpreted with caution.

Results

Descriptives

Modelling

Subjective social status (WE and BE) and income trajectory

One model per block of status (origin, own and children), without interactions: the income trajectory enters only as a main effect and the random-effects structure is (1 + ola_num | idencuesta) (random intercept and random time slope; no random slope for the WE status). The reference is Stable low.

  • Model 1: only the trend (linear and quadratic) and the trajectory.
  • Model 2 (base): adds the controls (ideology, sex, age and university education BE).
  • Models 3 to 5: to the base model, each block of status is added separately (origin, own and children, each with its WE and BE), not cumulatively.

Analysis sample: Ni = 7133 observations from Nj = 1210 persons.

NoteOptional: same table without political ideology

Same model as the table above, but without political ideology among the controls (same sample), to compare both.

Income trajectory × subjective social status (WE and BE)

One model per status (origin, own and children). Each model includes the controls, the six main effects of status, the income trajectory and the interaction of the trajectory with the WE and the BE of that column’s status. The slope of the interacted WE status is random: (1 + ola_num + X_we | idencuesta). Each likelihood-ratio test compares the model with interactions against a base model with the same random slope but no interactions.

NoteOptional: same table without political ideology

Same model as the table above, but without political ideology among the controls (same sample), to compare both.

Subjective mobility and income trajectory

Same structure as the previous section, but the subjective social status is replaced by the subjective mobility children - origin (mov_exp_og), decomposed into its within-person (WE) and between-person (BE, uncentred) components. There are no interactions and the random-effects structure is (1 + ola_num | idencuesta) (no random slope for the WE mobility). The reference is Stable low.

  • Model 1: only the trend (linear and quadratic) and the trajectory.
  • Model 2 (base): adds the controls (ideology, sex, age and university education BE).
  • Model 3: adds the mobility WE and BE to the base model.
NoteOptional: same table without political ideology

Same model as the table above, but without political ideology among the controls (same sample), to compare both.

Subjective mobility x income trajectory

Same structure as the Income trajectory × subjective social status section, but the subjective social status is replaced by the subjective mobility children - origin (mov_exp_og, WE and BE). The model includes the controls, the mobility main effects (WE and BE), the income trajectory and the interaction of the trajectory with the mobility WE and BE. The slope of the mobility WE is random: (1 + ola_num + mov_exp_og_cwc | idencuesta). Each likelihood-ratio test compares the model with interactions against a base model with the same random slope but no interactions.

NoteOptional: same table without political ideology

Same model as the table above, but without political ideology among the controls (same sample), to compare both.
