Abstract

Contemporary welfare states are characterized by the public provision of services such as education, health care, and pensions. Such services have experienced a process of commodification over the last years, whereby access to public goods increasingly depends on individual payment capacity, linked to the concept of market justice (Lane, 1986; Lindh, 2015). In this context, the study of market justice preferences acquires relevance as support for that type of distribution undermines the core of the welfare state.

Previous literature has found modest support for the role of class and status variables in shaping market justice preferences within a self-interest logic, usually from a static (cross-sectional) perspective. In the present paper, we include different measures of subjective social status, arguing that changes in this dimension operate above and beyond objective status measures.

Furthermore, we add a dynamic perspective, arguing that increasing objective and subjective status over time will be associated with stronger endorsement of market-justice preferences. This study draws on data from the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a representative panel of the Chilean urban population with six waves from 2016 to 2023 (N = 1326). This unique dataset offers, for the first time, the possibility to analyze variations across three dimensions of subjective social status: own, family of origin, and (future) offspring. The results of the multilevel longitudinal models indicate that subjective status is positively associated with market justice preferences (ceteris paribus). Moreover, the association between status variables and market justice preferences is far from being static, such that increasing status leads to stronger support for the role of individual payment capacity in accessing public goods.

Keywords: Longitudinal multilevel models, Market justice preferences, Social stratification, Social subjective status

Introduction

In recent decades, welfare states have undergone significant transformations in their institutional architecture, with social services increasingly coexisting with private alternatives (Busemeyer & Iversen, 2020). As a result, market dynamics have taken root around the world in services such as education, health care, and pension systems. Beyond institucional arrangements, market-oriented policies have not only formal consequences but also cultural ones, operating as moral projects in which individuals adopt such values as normative criteria (Fourcade & Healy, 2007). In this context, the concept of market justice has become key to understanding how people justify the fact that access to and the quality of social services depend on market criteria such as the ability to pay.

Some studies have examined how preferences for market justice relate to structural and cultural characteristics. On the structural side, objective status variables such as education, income, and occupational status have been related to a higher preference for market justice mechanisms, consisten with a rational approach (Lindh, 2015). In this area the results are rather inconsistent: while Lindh (2015) found that those from a higher social class tend to support market-oriented distribution, (Castillo et al., 2026) found no significant differences between class position and market justice regarding pensions. In this regard, it one’s position in the social structure does not seem sufficient on its own to account for such beliefs, leading to turn the look to complementary status variables, such as subjective status.

Subjective social status is understood as the self-assessment of one’s position within the social hierarchy. In contrast to objective status, it does not imply a categorization of the individual’s objective situation, but rather reflects perceived status in terms broader than occupation and income. So far, self-perceived status has played a minor role in the study of market preferences, despite the evidence of its role on perceptions of inequality and the demand for redistribution (Bar-Haim, 2018; Melli & Azzollini, 2025a, 2025b). As Hiltner & Cech (2024) points out, the subjective dimension may provide greater insights into attitudes and behaviors than objective measures. Thus, such perspective can enrich and complement the study of social status and market justice, broadening the explanatory framework of market justice.

The conceptualzation and operationalization of subjective social status has been mostly static. However, there is evidence that challenges this idea; Hiltner & Cech (2024) argues that the way people perceive their own movement through social hierarchies is more important than their movements within the labor market. In this way, self-perception can also reflect how people assess their trajectories—whether upward or downward—at a given moment, as well as how this perception changes over time. Within this framework, subjective mobility takes on particular significance, as it allows for the study of status in broader terms than occupational attainment—which is used to measure objective mobility—and, along the same lines, can provide greater insights than standardized measures (Duru-Bellat & Kieffer, 2008; Mijs et al., 2022).

This study aims to contribute to the literature on market justice and social stratification in two ways. First, through a rigorous examination of objective social status and, above all, subjective social status. Our data allows a comprehensive operationalization of subjective status, given that, in addition to one’s own subjective social status, we include that of the family of origin (past) and that of one’s children (future expectation). This allow us generating a series of variables to analytically represent subjective social mobility and consequently observe how they relate to market justice. Secondly, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to address the interaction between objective and subjective social status on market justice preferences and how they change over time. Our main argument is that subjective social status (both cross-sectionally and in terms of mobility) will carry the same or greater weight than objective status, shedding new light on how the perception of one’s own life trajectory plays a fundamental role in market-oriented criteria of justice.

This research is particularly relevant in Chile, a country characterized by a model marked by high levels of inequality and rigid social mobility (Flores et al., 2020; Torche, 2014), the foundations of which took shape during the civil-military dictatorship that the country endured between 1973 and 1989 (Madariaga, 2020). Similarly, there is a high degree of privatization in areas of social reproduction, coupled with a strong dependence on the state (Boccardo, 2020). In addition, we have the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a unique project in Latin America that tracked and recorded people’s characteristics and beliefs between 2016 and 2023, allowing us to study change over time. Thus, these conditions make the study of Chile a productive case for understanding the extent to which status (both objective and subjective) determines preferences for market justice.

Data, Variables and Methods

Data

This research draws on the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a national panel study fielded between 2016 and 2023 to examine attitudes, emotions, and behaviors among adults aged 18 to 75 in relation to social cohesion and conflict. ELSOC uses a probabilistic, stratified, clustered, multistage sampling design covering urban centers and small towns. The sampling frame is proportionally stratified into six categories of urban population size; within these strata, households were selected through multistage procedures based on 1,067 sampled blocks.

ELSOC has been administered annually since 2016, except in 2020 when fieldwork was suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic. For this study, we use waves 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2022, and 2023. The 2021 wave is excluded because the questionnaire version omitted key variables for the analysis. By wave 6, cumulative panel attrition was approximately 40%; we therefore restrict the analysis to a balanced panel, yielding a two-level data set of N = 6,699 observations nested within N = 1,203 individuals. Additional documentation on sampling, attrition, and weighting is available from the ELSOC study team, and the data are publicly accessible through the ELSOC Dataverse.

Variables

Market justice preferences

The dependent variable in this study is preferences for market justice (MJP). This construct is measured by three items that capture how strongly individuals endorse conditioning access to pensions, education and health care on income. The original items asks: “Is it fair that high-income individuals have better (education, health or pensions) than lower-income individuals?”. The response options range from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), so that higher values indicate stronger support for market-justified inequality in the provision of social services.

We construct the MJP index as the mean of the three items, which ranges from 1 to 5. The index is computed only for respondents with valid answers on all three items and is treated as a continuous variable in the models. Figure 1 shows its distribution by wave.

Social Subjective Status

Subjective social status is measured with three ELSOC items in which respondents place themselves, their family of origin and their children on a scale from 0 (“lowest”) to 10 (“highest”) of the social ladder. The first item captures the respondent’s own current position, the second the position of the family of origin, and the third the position where respondents expect their children to be located. Higher values indicate a higher perceived position in all three cases. The three measures are treated as continuous variables and, in the models, are decomposed into their within-person and between-person components. In addition, we construct an SSS index as the mean of the three measures, which is used as a control in the models that include the trajectory slope of subjective mobility (see below).

Social Subjective Mobility

Based on the original variables of subjective social status (origin, self, and children), various variables were generated from all the combinations allowed by these three items. The composite variables operationalize subjective mobility as follows: \(t_{2} - t_{1}\). Thus, positive values indicate upward subjective mobility. Additionally, with the aim of exploring subjective mobility in greater depth, a variable was constructed to represent an individual’s trajectory of subjective social status, based on a linear regression that predicts a slope (which represents the trajectory) from the three original subjective social statuses.

Social Objective Status

Objective social status is measured through education and household income. Education is the respondent’s highest educational level, reported on a ten-category scale ranging from no schooling to postgraduate studies. We use it in three versions: (1) years of education, obtained by assigning to each level the average years of schooling; (2) four categories (primary or less, secondary, technical, and university or postgraduate); and (3) a dichotomous indicator of university education (university or postgraduate vs. lower levels). Years of education were descomposed into their within-person and between-person effects, while the categorical and dichotomous measures are treated as time-invariant and only include a between-person component.

Income is the total monthly household income reported by the respondent. When the amount is missing, we impute the midpoint of the income bracket answered in the follow-up question. Household income is deflated to constant December 2023 prices using the consumer price index and divided by the square root of household size to obtain an equivalised income, which is used in three versions: (1) its natural logarithm; (2) three groups defined by within-wave deciles (bottom: deciles 1-4, middle: deciles 5-9, top: decile 10); and (3) a dichotomous indicator for the top decile. The logarithm of equivalised income is decomposed into its within-person and between-person effects, while the categorical and dichotomous measures only include a between-person component.

Controls

Based on previous research (Castillo et al., 2025; Jaime-Castillo, 2013; Lindh, 2015), the statistical models include sociodemographic and attitudinal controls to account for potential compositional effects in the population. We adjust for sex (1 = male, 2 = female), age (in years) and political identification (1 = left, 2 = centre, 3 = right, 4 = no identification).

Analytical Strategy

The analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we describe the distribution of each status variable by wave and examine the bivariate association between the status measures and market justice preferences using Spearman correlations, computed within each wave and, for the within/between-person components, on the pooled sample. Second, we estimate linear longitudinal multilevel models for the market justice preferences (MJP) index, which is the mean of the pension, education and health items (range 1-5). Because the index is an average of several items and is treated as a continuous outcome, we use linear mixed models rather than cumulative link models. This approach accounts for the hierarchical structure of the panel data, with repeated observations (Level 1) nested within individuals (Level 2), and enables the decomposition of the variance of the predictors into within-person (WE) and between-person (BE) components, allowing us to distinguish temporal changes within individuals from stable differences between individuals (Bell et al., 2019; Singer, 2003). All descriptive figures, correlations and models are based on the same analytical sample (complete cases on all analysis variables; N = 6,699 observations from N = 1,203 individuals).

Within-Person and Between-Person Random Effects Models

Following the recommendations of Bell & Jones (2015), we decompose time-varying predictors into their within-person and between-person components using person-mean centering:

\[ X_{WE_{it}} = X_{it} - \bar{X}_i \]

\[ X_{BE_i} = \bar{X}_i \]

where \(X_{it}\) represents the observed value for individual \(i\) at time \(t\), \(\bar{X}_i\) is the person-specific mean across all observations, \(X_{WE_{it}}\) captures within-person deviations from the individual mean (representing temporal fluctuations), and \(X_{BE_i}\) represents stable between-person differences (time-invariant characteristics). The decomposition is applied to the continuous measures: the three subjective status measures (own, family of origin and children), the subjective mobility measures, years of education and log equivalised household income.

For the dichotomous and categorical measures of objective status only a between-person component is included, since they vary little within individuals. For the dichotomous measures, the BE component indicates whether the person was ever observed with a university degree or in the top income decile. For the categorical measures (education in four categories and income in three groups), the BE component is the person’s modal category across waves, with ties assigned to the higher category; the reference categories are primary education or less and the bottom income group (deciles 1-4), respectively.

This decomposition allows us to distinguish two theoretically distinct processes: (1) Within-person effects capture how changes in an individual’s subjective and objective status over time relate to changes in their market justice preferences, controlling for stable individual characteristics. (2) Between-person effects capture how individuals with different average levels of subjective and objective status differ in their market justice preferences, net of temporal fluctuations.

Model Specification

The general form of our linear mixed model is:

\[ \text{MJP}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{wave}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{wave}^2_{it} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_3' X_{WE_{it}} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_4' X_{BE_i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' Z_i + u_{0i} + u_{1i} \text{wave}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

where \(\text{MJP}_{it}\) is the market justice preferences index for individual \(i\) at wave \(t\); \(\beta_0\) is the fixed intercept; \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) capture the linear and quadratic time trends (wave is coded 1 to 6, since the 2020-2021 waves are not available); \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_3\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_4\) are the within-person and between-person effects of the status measures included in each model; \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) are the coefficients of the controls \(Z_i\) (sex, age and political identification), which enter only in the models that include controls; \(u_{0i}\) is the random intercept (capturing unobserved individual heterogeneity); \(u_{1i}\) is a random slope for wave (allowing individual-specific temporal trajectories); and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is the residual term, \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\).

The random effects are assumed to follow a bivariate normal distribution:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} u_{0i} \\ u_{1i} \end{pmatrix} \sim N \begin{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2_{u_0} & \sigma_{u_{01}} \\ \sigma_{u_{01}} & \sigma^2_{u_1} \end{pmatrix} \end{pmatrix} \]

Model Building Strategy

The same specification (time terms and random effects) is kept in all models, and the status measures are entered in blocks. Within each block, we first enter each measure on its own, then add the controls, and finally estimate a model including all the measures of the block:

  1. Subjective social status (Models 1-8): own, family-of-origin and children’s subjective status (WE/BE), each entered separately (Models 1-3) and with controls (Models 4-6); a model with the family-of-origin and children’s status and controls (Model 7); and a model with the three measures and controls (Model 8).
  2. Subjective social mobility (Models 9-25): each mobility measure (own - origin, own - mean origin, children - own, intergenerational, children - origin, the trajectory slope and the trajectory curve; WE/BE) entered separately (Models 9-16; the slope and the curve are entered each on their own and together) and with controls (Models 17-24), and a model with all measures and controls (Model 25). The models that include the trajectory slope or curve also control for the SSS index (WE/BE). Since the mobility measures are linear combinations of the three subjective status measures, the full model is rank deficient and the aliased terms are dropped.
  3. Objective social status (Models 26-38): education and income, each in its three operationalizations (dichotomous BE; four categories for education and three groups for income, BE; and continuous, years of education and log income, WE/BE). Each operationalization is entered separately for education (Models 26-28) and for income (Models 29-31); the same models are estimated with controls (Models 32-37); and a final model includes all the education and income measures with controls (Model 38).
  4. Subjective mobility and objective status (Models 39-56): each of the mobility measures children - own, intergenerational, children - origin and trajectory slope (WE/BE) is entered together with education and income, first with continuous measures (years of education and log income, WE/BE; Models 39-42), then with controls (Models 43-46), and finally with all the variables and controls (Model 47). As in the previous block, the models that include the trajectory slope also control for the SSS index (WE/BE). The same sequence is repeated replacing the education and income measures with dichotomous education (BE) and income in three categories (BE; Models 48-56).

All models are estimated by maximum likelihood (not REML), so that models with different fixed effects can be compared. Models are unweighted and are estimated on the analytical sample described above using the lmer() function from the lme4 package in R, and the results are presented in tables with the regression coefficients and their standard errors (texreg).

Proposal for final models

Based on separate models of subjective social status, subjective mobility, and objective status, a final proposal for the model is presented. In these subsections, the following variables were selected: subjective mobility between one’s own status and that of one’s children; intergenerational mobility; mobility between one’s status of origin and that of one’s children; and, finally, social trajectory. These were the variables that were shown to have some effect on market justice preferences, and therefore they were prioritized over the others. Furthermore, a model structure is generated that includes two modeling approaches for the objective variables: one using dichotomized educational attainment and income divided into three categories, and another using years of education and logarithmic income. This distinction was made to incorporate the WE effect of education and income into the model, which serves as a proxy for objective mobility.
